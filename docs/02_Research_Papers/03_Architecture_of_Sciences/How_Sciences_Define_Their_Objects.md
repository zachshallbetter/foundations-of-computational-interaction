**How Sciences Define Their Objects**

*Toward a General Architecture of Scientific Explanation*

**Formal comparative research paper**

July 2026

# Abstract

This paper asks whether mature sciences share a general architecture by
which they define and organize primary objects of explanation. A
comparative analysis of eighteen natural, social, formal, engineering,
and interdisciplinary fields is combined with a review of major accounts
of scientific explanation, theory change, scientific objects, natural
kinds, mechanisms, levels, experimentation, and disciplinary formation.
The comparison does not support a universal object-centered template.
Disciplines may be organized around entities, processes, relations,
problem classes, methods, or constructed systems; historical sciences
need not make precise novel predictions; and mechanism is not the
exclusive form of explanation. A weaker but stable architecture does
emerge. Mature sciences stabilize inquiry by coordinating seven
functions: (1) a delimited domain of phenomena and explanatory targets;
(2) units, levels, and scope conditions; (3) ontological commitments
concerning relevant entities, properties, relations, and processes; (4)
epistemic access through observations, measurements, instruments,
records, and interventions; (5) public representations such as
classifications, models, diagrams, equations, and simulations; (6)
explanatory dependencies expressed as laws, mechanisms, causes,
structures, functions, histories, computations, or statistical
relations; and (7) success constraints governing evidence, comparison,
prediction or retrodiction, intervention, unification, and error
correction. These functions are stabilized socially through exemplars,
standards, institutions, and trained communities, but community
organization is a condition of disciplinary persistence rather than a
component of every scientific explanation. The architecture is therefore
functional and relational, not a fixed inventory of objects. Applied as
a worked example, interaction is structurally compatible with scientific
treatment because it can supply phenomena, units, measurements,
representations, and candidate dependencies. Structural compatibility
alone does not establish theoretical autonomy or justify a new
discipline. The answer is qualifiedly affirmative: mature sciences
exhibit a general architecture of stabilization, but not a single
universal ontology or explanatory form.

Primary contribution: mature sciences do not share one kind of object,
but they do converge on a minimal architecture that stabilizes
explanatory targets by coordinating ontology, epistemic access,
representation, explanatory dependence, and evidential success.

# Introduction

Scientific disciplines are often described as if each were defined by a
noun: physics studies matter and energy, biology studies life,
linguistics studies language, and psychology studies mind and behavior.
Such formulations are useful administratively but weak philosophically.
They obscure disagreements over what the named object is, which level is
primary, how it becomes observable, what forms of explanation count, and
why some fields organized around methods or problems nevertheless
function as sciences.

The research question is therefore not whether disciplines can be
assigned labels. It is whether mature sciences repeatedly solve a common
organizational problem: how to select phenomena as explananda, delimit a
domain, posit or construct objects, obtain epistemic access, represent
dependencies, and evaluate explanations. A genuine general architecture
must survive counterexamples from historical, formal, social,
engineering, and interdisciplinary sciences. It must also distinguish a
scientific object from a scientific discipline. An object may be studied
by several disciplines, while one discipline may address several
objects.

The paper uses \'scientific object\' in a deliberately
non-realist-neutral sense: a relatively stable target of systematic
inquiry that is individuated through theory and practice and can support
intersubjective claims. The definition does not require that every
object be directly observable, a natural kind, mind-independent in the
strongest metaphysical sense, or unique to one discipline.
\'Explanandum\' denotes the phenomenon, regularity, event, pattern,
capacity, or outcome to be explained. \'Architecture\' denotes the
smallest recurring set of functions needed to organize sustained
explanation, not a historical sequence every science must follow.

Methodologically, the analysis combines structured comparison with
philosophical synthesis. The disciplinary survey samples natural,
historical, social, formal, applied, and interdisciplinary fields. For
each field it identifies the dominant target, unit, ontology, relations,
mechanisms or alternative dependencies, evidence, and predictive forms.
The philosophical review treats competing accounts as constraints rather
than selecting one school in advance. The proposed architecture is
retained only where it describes every surveyed field without forcing
all sciences into the image of experimental physics.

# Problem Statement

A theory of scientific organization can fail in two opposite ways. It
can be too strong, requiring a single natural object, universal laws,
controlled experiments, or novel predictions and thereby excluding
geology, evolutionary biology, parts of astronomy, anthropology,
computer science, and design-oriented sciences. Or it can be too weak,
listing generic features such as evidence and explanation that
distinguish little. The problem is to identify recurring structure
without erasing genuine pluralism.

Four distinctions control the analysis:

-   Scientific object versus object of a discipline: electrons can be
    objects of physics, chemistry, materials science, and engineering;
    no ownership relation follows.

-   Ontology versus methodology: what a field claims exists or matters
    is distinct from how it obtains evidence.

-   Explanandum versus unit of analysis: a field may explain population
    change while measuring organisms, genes, or demographic rates.

-   Discipline versus research program: disciplines are durable
    institutional formations containing multiple programs, whereas
    programs can cross disciplinary boundaries.

# Current Theories of Scientific Organization

## Logical structure and explanatory relations

Hempel and Oppenheim treated scientific explanation as subsumption of
the explanandum under general laws and initial conditions. The model
made explicit the relation between explanans and explanandum, but its
symmetry with prediction and dependence on laws proved too restrictive.
It remains important because it establishes that explanation requires
more than naming a target: a field must articulate relations that make
the target expectable or intelligible (Hempel & Oppenheim, 1948).

Subsequent accounts pluralized explanatory dependence. Kitcher
emphasized unification through a small set of argument patterns; Salmon
emphasized causal relevance and processes; Woodward linked causal
explanation to invariance under interventions; and mechanists
characterized mechanisms as organized entities and activities productive
of regular changes (Kitcher, 1981; Salmon, 1984; Woodward, 2003;
Machamer, Darden, & Craver, 2000). These theories disagree about
explanation, which is evidence against treating one form - especially
mechanism - as architecturally mandatory.

## Paradigms, research programs, and disciplinary matrices

Kuhn\'s mature science is stabilized by shared exemplars, symbolic
generalizations, models, and values rather than by a definition of
subject matter alone. His later \'disciplinary matrix\' is especially
relevant: a research community coordinates what problems are legitimate,
what solutions look like, and what standards govern puzzle solving.
Scientific revolutions can reorganize both ontology and explanatory
targets (Kuhn, 1962/1970). Lakatos shifts the unit from paradigms to
research programs with a relatively protected hard core and a changing
protective belt; progress is judged by theoretical and empirical
development over time (Lakatos, 1978). Neither account yields a
universal list of objects, but both show that objects are stabilized
inside structured practices.

## Experimental objects and epistemic things

Hacking argues that scientific entities acquire special warrant through
intervention and manipulation, not representation alone. Experimental
practice can stabilize entities before theory converges (Hacking, 1983).
Rheinberger\'s \'epistemic things\' are materially embodied targets
whose relevant properties are not yet fully known; experimental systems
generate differences that progressively articulate them (Rheinberger,
1997). Daston and Galison show that scientific objectivity itself has
historical forms tied to representational practices and trained judgment
(Daston & Galison, 2007). These approaches make instruments and
representations constitutive of object stabilization without implying
that instruments create every referent.

## Natural kinds, classifications, and constructed targets

Essentialist natural kinds cannot organize all sciences. Biological
species, psychiatric categories, social classifications, and
computational types often have historical, functional, cluster-based, or
engineered unity. Boyd\'s homeostatic property cluster account, Dupré\'s
pluralism, and Hacking\'s analysis of interactive human kinds show that
categories may be stable enough for inquiry without possessing classical
essences (Boyd, 1991; Dupré, 1993; Hacking, 1999). Classification is
therefore an epistemic achievement to be evaluated, not a universal
discovery of fixed essences.

## Levels, mechanisms, and perspectival organization

Marr distinguished computational, algorithmic, and implementational
levels; explanatory questions change with level. Craver treats levels as
component-mechanism relations rather than a single universal hierarchy.
Wimsatt emphasizes robustness across independent means of detection and
analysis. Giere\'s perspectivism and Cartwright\'s attention to
capacities caution that models represent selected aspects under
conditions, not complete mirrors of the world (Marr, 1982; Wimsatt,
2007; Craver, 2007; Giere, 2006; Cartwright, 1989). A general
architecture must therefore include explicit units, levels, and scope
conditions.

## Communities, boundaries, and institutional emergence

Fleck described thought collectives and styles; Gieryn analyzed boundary
work by which communities demarcate science; Abbott showed that
disciplines organize through shifting oppositions and problem
structures; and Stichweh connected differentiation to specialized
communication and institutional reproduction (Fleck, 1935/1979; Gieryn,
1983; Abbott, 2001; Stichweh, 1992). These accounts explain disciplinary
persistence and jurisdiction. They do not imply that communities may
define arbitrary objects successfully: empirical resistance, technical
performance, and cross-context reliability constrain stabilization.

# Recurring Architectural Structures

The survey converges, but not on the candidate inventory in its original
form. Object, observer, observation, relationship, mechanism,
representation, explanation, prediction, and evidence are not equally
fundamental. \'Object\' is often plural or processual; \'observer\' is
sometimes distributed or endogenous; \'mechanism\' is one explanatory
form among several; and \'prediction\' may be replaced by retrodiction,
constraint, classification, proof, intervention, or qualitative
expectation. The stable commonality is functional.

The architecture can be expressed compactly. A scientific research
formation stabilizes a target T when a community can (a) individuate
instances of T at specified units and levels, (b) obtain disciplined
epistemic access to them, (c) represent their relevant features
publicly, (d) propose dependencies that answer contrastive explanatory
questions, and (e) expose those claims to shared error-detection and
comparison. Ontology, method, and representation are mutually
constraining rather than linearly ordered.

# Counterexamples

## Method-centered fields

Statistics, microscopy, spectroscopy, and parts of computer science
appear organized around methods rather than natural objects. They do not
refute the architecture because their explanatory targets are problem
classes - uncertainty, inference, detection, computation, or
measurement - and their objects are often formal or constructed. They do
refute the claim that every discipline requires one bounded natural
kind.

## Historical sciences

Geology, evolutionary biology, cosmology, archaeology, and historical
linguistics often explain singular or path-dependent outcomes. Their
strongest achievements may be retrodictive consilience, constraint of
possible histories, and risky traces expected under one history but not
another. They refute prediction symmetry as a universal criterion, not
the need for success constraints.

## Interpretive and reflexive sciences

Anthropology, sociology, and parts of linguistics investigate agents
whose classifications and interpretations can alter the phenomena.
Hacking\'s interactive kinds and reflexive methodology show that the
observer-observed relation cannot always be externalized. These fields
still establish units, evidence, representations, dependencies, and
criticism, but explanation may include reasons, meanings, institutions,
and looping effects alongside causes.

## Formal and design sciences

Mathematics and theoretical computer science prove consequences within
formal systems; design sciences study artificial systems partly by
constructing them. Their evidence includes proof and artifact
performance, which differ from observation of unaided nature. Simon\'s
sciences of the artificial show that purpose, specification, and
synthesis can be legitimate explanatory contexts. These cases require
\'epistemic access\' and \'success constraints\' broad enough to include
proof, verification, and constructive demonstration.

## Fields without a single paradigm

Psychology, sociology, anthropology, HCI, and cognitive science contain
multiple ontologies and explanatory traditions. They remain disciplines
because institutional and problem continuity can coexist with
theoretical pluralism. Kuhnian consensus is therefore a degree of
maturation within programs, not a necessary global state for an entire
discipline.

# Proposed General Architecture

No accepted philosophy-of-science theory currently specifies how all
sciences define their objects. Existing accounts explain different
dimensions: logical relations, causal dependence, mechanisms,
unification, paradigms, research programs, experimental manipulation,
classification, perspectival representation, and community formation.
The present proposal is therefore a comparative synthesis, not a
discovered consensus theory.

## Relations among components

1.  Target selection is theory-laden but empirically constrained.
    Phenomena become salient against prior questions and
    representations, yet recalcitrant observations can force
    reclassification.

2.  Units and boundaries mediate ontology. The same domain can support
    genes, organisms, populations, or ecosystems as units without one
    eliminating the others.

3.  Epistemic access co-produces object stability. Instruments, records,
    and interventions determine which properties can be reliably
    discriminated, while successful manipulation and robustness can
    increase realist warrant.

4.  Representations make comparison possible but are not identical with
    their targets. Multiple representations may be equivalent for one
    purpose and incompatible for another.

5.  Explanatory dependencies answer contrastive questions under scope
    conditions. No universal dependency type is privileged across all
    sciences.

6.  Success constraints close the loop by selecting, revising, or
    rejecting ontologies, measurements, representations, and
    explanations.

7.  Communities stabilize the loop through shared practices, but
    stabilization remains provisional because standards, instruments,
    and targets can change.

The worked example establishes structural compatibility only.
Interaction can be individuated as a family of explanatory targets,
observed through multiple methods, represented formally, and related to
candidate dependencies. However, these conditions are permissive: many
legitimate research objects satisfy them while remaining shared across
disciplines. To establish theoretical independence, an
interaction-centered program would need to show stable interaction-level
variables or invariants that produce explanatory and predictive gains
not obtainable by integrating cognitive, ecological, informational,
social, control, and computational accounts.

A falsifying result would be equally clear: if every robust interaction
phenomenon can be explained without residual loss by existing component
and relational theories, and if proposed interaction-level constructs
add no comparative prediction, intervention guidance, or unification,
interaction remains a cross-disciplinary object rather than an
autonomous theoretical domain.

# Threats to Validity

-   Selection bias: eighteen fields cannot represent every science.
    Medicine, mathematics, statistics, climate science, neuroscience,
    archaeology, and engineering could shift emphasis, although the
    counterexample analysis incorporates several of their organizational
    forms.

-   Internal heterogeneity: each table entry compresses contested
    subfields. The architecture is intended to describe recurring
    functions, not adjudicate each discipline\'s ontology.

-   Presentist reconstruction: contemporary disciplinary structures may
    conceal historical alternatives. Kuhnian and historical work partly
    corrects this, but the survey is not a full genealogy.

-   Philosophical underdetermination: realism, instrumentalism,
    perspectivism, and social constructivism permit different
    interpretations of stabilization. The framework is designed to
    remain neutral where the comparative result does not require
    resolution.

-   Functional abstraction: the seven components may appear inevitable
    because they are broadly formulated. Their value depends on
    discriminating what is not universal - single objects, mechanisms,
    external observers, experiments, and prediction - and on specifying
    relations among the retained functions.

-   Application contamination: prior interest in interaction could bias
    the derived framework. The architecture was therefore formulated and
    tested against the full disciplinary sample before interaction was
    evaluated.

-   Evidence asymmetry: foundational philosophy sources are not a
    systematic bibliometric sample. The paper reviews influential
    competing positions and records disagreement rather than claiming
    exhaustive consensus.

# Discussion

The central result is a qualified convergence. Mature disciplines do not
determine their primary objects by discovering a single metaphysically
given partition of reality. Nor are objects merely arbitrary products of
communities. They are stabilized through a recurrent coupling of
explanatory demand, ontological articulation, epistemic access, public
representation, and error-sensitive comparison. This coupling can
converge on entities such as electrons, processes such as evolution,
relations such as ecological interaction, formal structures such as
algorithms, or historically constituted practices.

The primary object of a discipline is therefore best understood as a
stabilized explanatory target system rather than a noun. \'System\' here
does not imply systems theory; it denotes the linked set of targets,
units, ontological commitments, access procedures, representations,
dependencies, and standards that make sustained inquiry possible. A
discipline may host several such systems, and one system may cross
disciplinary boundaries.

The distinction between object and discipline resolves apparent
anomalies. Cognition is studied by psychology, neuroscience,
linguistics, computer science, anthropology, and cognitive science.
Information is studied in physics, biology, computer science,
communication, and information science. Interaction similarly may be a
primary object of particular research programs without being the
exclusive object of HCI or the basis of an independent discipline.
Disciplinary autonomy requires additional sociological and theoretical
conditions: a sustained problem agenda, specialist training,
institutions, journals, instruments or datasets, and demonstrable
explanatory gains.

The architecture also reframes scientific emergence. New sciences are
not born when a label is coined. They emerge when previously dispersed
anomalies or questions become a coherent target domain; units and
boundaries become reproducible; ontologies support cumulative models;
epistemic techniques make relevant differences detectable; explanations
outperform competitors; and a community preserves the resulting
standards. Instruments and communities can lead this process, but they
cannot substitute indefinitely for explanatory success.

No single philosophical account is sufficient. Hempel clarifies the
explanandum-explanans relation; Kuhn and Lakatos explain organized
continuity and change; Hacking and Rheinberger explain experimental
stabilization; natural-kind debates explain classificatory pluralism;
mechanism and intervention accounts explain difference-making in many
empirical sciences; unification accounts explain cross-domain
compression; and sociology of science explains institutional
persistence. The proposed architecture is the intersection of these
contributions after counterexamples remove non-universal requirements.

# Conclusion

Yes, there is a general architecture by which mature sciences define and
organize primary objects of explanation, but it is not a universal
object ontology or a single theory of explanation. Mature sciences
recurrently coordinate seven functions: a phenomenal domain and
explanatory targets; units, levels, boundaries, and scope; ontological
commitments; epistemic access; public representations; explanatory
dependencies; and success and error constraints. Research communities,
instruments, exemplars, and institutions stabilize this architecture
over time.

The architecture is minimal because removing any retained function
prevents cumulative explanatory inquiry: without targets there is no
explanandum; without units and scope claims cannot be compared; without
ontology relevant differences are unspecified; without epistemic access
claims cannot answer to evidence; without representations they cannot
circulate or accumulate; without dependencies description does not
become explanation; and without success constraints error cannot be
discriminated from progress. Conversely, a single natural object, an
external observer, experimental manipulation, mechanism, and novel
prediction are not universal requirements.

Interaction is structurally compatible with this architecture. That
result supports treating interaction as a possible primary object of
particular scientific explanations. It does not establish that
interaction is theoretically autonomous, that one ontology is correct,
or that a dedicated discipline is justified. Those stronger conclusions
require comparative explanatory gains that have not been demonstrated by
structural analysis alone.
