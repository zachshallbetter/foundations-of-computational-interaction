# Abstract

This paper asks whether interaction itself can serve as a primary object
of scientific explanation rather than merely a property of humans,
machines, communication, cognition, or environments. It compares the
scientific structure of twelve disciplines, reviews major ways
interaction is conceptualized, and evaluates formal traditions that
model interaction directly. The analysis distinguishes three claims that
are often conflated: that interaction is real and measurable; that it
can be selected as the focal explanandum; and that it requires an
independent discipline. Relational and process-centered sciences such as
ecology, linguistics, cybernetics, and systems theory show that a
scientific object need not be a substance. Interaction meets the
descriptive requirements of a scientific object when specified as a
bounded trajectory of reciprocal causal influence among distinguishable
systems: events can be observed, variables measured, mechanisms
proposed, models falsified, and predictions tested. However, ecological
psychology, dynamical systems, control theory, distributed cognition,
activity theory, conversation analysis, HCI, computational interaction,
process calculi, game theory, and reinforcement learning already supply
much of the necessary ontology and formal machinery. No reviewed
evidence demonstrates a general interaction-specific mechanism that is
irreducible to a principled combination of those frameworks, and no
cross-domain benchmark establishes superior predictions from an
independent theory. The evidence therefore supports treating interaction
as a primary scientific phenomenon and organizing a dedicated
comparative theoretical program, but not yet an autonomous science. The
paper ends with the minimum ontology and empirical tests needed to
decide whether theoretical independence emerges.

**Keywords:** interaction; scientific object; HCI; cybernetics; systems
theory; ecological psychology; computational interaction; ontology;
process theory

# 1. Introduction

The expression "interaction" appears across nearly every science.
Particles interact through fields; molecules interact through bonds and
reactions; organisms interact with environments; speakers interact
through language; people interact with machines; and processes interact
through communication and synchronization. Ubiquity, however, does not
establish scientific independence. A term may identify a mechanism
inside another theory, a relation among already accepted entities, a
descriptive label, or a genuine phenomenon whose regularities warrant
direct explanation.

The central question is therefore not whether interaction matters. It is
whether interaction can be made the subject of explanations in which the
interaction trajectory, rather than either participant considered
separately, is the principal dependent object. This requires a stricter
test than demonstrating that interaction is observable. Scientific work
routinely studies relations without granting each relation a separate
discipline. Conversely, ecology, linguistics, and cybernetics
demonstrate that a field may legitimately organize around relations,
processes, or patterns rather than material substances.

This paper distinguishes a primary scientific object from an autonomous
scientific domain. A primary object is a stable class of phenomena
selected as the focal target of measurement and explanation. An
autonomous domain additionally requires theoretical independence:
characteristic mechanisms, explanatory questions, and cumulative models
that cannot be replaced without loss by existing disciplines. The
distinction permits a positive answer to the object question without
presupposing a new discipline.

The paper\'s single contribution is a conditional determination:
interaction is scientifically admissible as a primary object, but the
evidence presently supports a distinct theoretical program rather than a
distinct discipline. That determination is based on comparative
structure, not on the existence of a community or the novelty of
terminology.

# 2. What Constitutes a Scientific Object?

A scientific object is not simply anything that exists. It is an
empirically tractable target stabilized by definitions, instruments,
measurement conventions, models, and explanatory practices. Electrons,
genes, species, grammatical structures, memory, and ecosystems differ
ontologically, yet each becomes scientifically usable through operations
that identify instances, distinguish variables, and connect observations
to mechanisms.

Four conditions are necessary. First, an object must have identity
conditions: researchers must be able to state what counts as an instance
and where the object begins and ends. Second, it must support
observation or warranted inference through an explicit observation
model. Third, it must enter reproducible explanatory relations: changes
in specified conditions should imply differences in measurements or
distributions. Fourth, theories about it must be vulnerable to evidence.
These conditions do not require perfect consensus or direct visibility.
They require disciplined correspondence among definitions, observations,
and claims.

A stronger condition applies when a proposed object is used to justify a
new scientific domain. It must supply incremental explanatory power. Its
characteristic variables or mechanisms must outperform, simplify, unify,
or otherwise materially improve on established accounts. Institutional
novelty, terminological fragmentation, and interdisciplinary
inconvenience do not meet that standard.

For this study, interaction qualifies as a primary object only if it can
be individuated independently of a named interface, measured as more
than a list of participant properties, and modeled such that the
trajectory has explanatory properties not captured by a static
description of its participants. It qualifies as the basis of an
independent domain only if these models require indispensable constructs
or mechanisms absent from existing theories.

The comparison yields three conclusions. First, mature sciences are not
distinguished solely by ontological type. Physics and chemistry
emphasize substances and states; ecology emphasizes relations and
populations; linguistics emphasizes structured capacities and products;
cybernetics emphasizes circular processes. A relational phenomenon is
therefore not disqualified from primary status.

Second, interaction is already a primary term inside several
disciplines. Ecology directly studies organism-environment and
organism-organism relations. Human factors officially defines itself
around interactions among humans and system elements. HCI explicitly
studies the human-computer interaction process. Cybernetics treats
communication and feedback loops as the central phenomenon. Interaction
is therefore not scientifically homeless.

Third, disciplines differ less by exclusive ownership of entities than
by characteristic explanatory questions, boundary conventions,
mechanisms, and evidence. Biology uses physical and chemical entities
but asks biological questions. Ecology uses organisms already studied by
biology but makes their relations and population-level consequences
focal. The relevant test for interaction is consequently whether making
the trajectory focal produces distinctive, cumulative explanations that
are not merely renamed ecology, cognition, control, communication, or
system dynamics.

# 4. How Interaction Is Currently Treated

## 4.1 Competing ontological roles

-   **Object:** The interaction episode or trajectory is itself bounded,
    compared, and explained. Common in conversation analysis,
    interaction analysis, HCI studies, and some computational models.

-   **Process:** Interaction is reciprocal influence unfolding over
    time. This is dominant in dynamical systems, ecological psychology,
    enactivism, cybernetics, and situated action.

-   **Relationship:** Interaction denotes a relation between entities,
    often without asserting a separate object. Ecology, sociology, and
    network analysis frequently use this form.

-   **Mechanism:** Interaction is a causal means through which outcomes
    arise: molecular collision, feedback, social influence, signaling,
    or human-machine coordination.

-   **Property:** A system is described as interactive, coupled,
    responsive, or open. The property belongs to a system architecture
    rather than to an episode.

-   **Outcome:** Interaction is measured through engagement,
    coordination, influence, or exchange produced by prior conditions.

-   **Metaphor:** "Dialogue," "conversation," "partnership," and
    "ecology" are sometimes transferred to systems without the
    commitments of the source theory. Metaphorical use is productive for
    design but insufficient for scientific identity.

## 4.2 Philosophical assumptions

Representational accounts treat interaction as information exchange
among systems that possess internal states, goals, and mappings.
Classical cognitive HCI, information-processing psychology, agent
models, and many semantic ontologies take this form. Their strength is
decomposition and prediction; their risk is treating context and
material organization as inputs to an otherwise self-contained
processor.

Ecological and enactive accounts treat interaction as
organism-environment coupling in which perception and action are
co-defined. Affordances are relational possibilities for action rather
than properties of an object alone. Their strength is direct treatment
of embodiment and environment; their risk is underspecifying symbolic
interpretation, institutional structure, or computational architecture.

Dynamical and cybernetic accounts treat interaction as coupled state
change, feedback, regulation, and mutual constraint. They provide
precise temporal and mathematical tools. Their risk is explanatory
thinness when goals, meanings, norms, and historical practices are
compressed into state variables without an account of their
constitution.

Situated, distributed, and activity-theoretical accounts place
interaction within material, social, historical, and organizational
systems. They explain how action is mediated by artifacts, rules,
communities, plans, representations, and division of labor. Their
strength is boundary expansion; their risk is difficulty producing
compact cross-domain predictions.

Pragmatic and interactional linguistic accounts treat meaning as
publicly achieved through sequences of action, inference, turn-taking,
repair, and common ground. They show that an utterance\'s meaning is not
exhausted by its encoded content. Their risk, for a general interaction
science, is domain restriction to communicative and socially accountable
action.

Formal computer science treats interaction as communication,
synchronization, observation, and response among computational processes
or between system and environment. Process calculi, automata, game
semantics, protocol models, and coalgebra provide
representation-independent descriptions with strong compositionality and
verification. They generally abstract away from human perception,
learning, embodiment, and culture.

# 5. Systematic Literature Review

## 5.1 Review design

The review was structured as a theory-oriented scoping review rather
than a meta-analysis. Searches targeted the terms interaction theory,
interaction ontology, formal interaction model, interaction grammar,
interaction primitives, interaction semantics, interaction machines,
computational interaction, agent-environment coupling, social
interaction, conversation analysis, process algebra, game semantics,
cybernetics, ecological psychology, distributed cognition, situated
action, activity theory, and human-computer interaction. Sources were
selected when they proposed definitions, ontologies, formal mechanisms,
or explicit boundaries, rather than merely reporting a designed system.

Priority was given to primary or field-defining sources: Wiener on
cybernetics; von Bertalanffy on general systems; Gibson on affordances
and ecological information; Card, Moran, and Newell on quantitative HCI;
Suchman on situated action; Hutchins on distributed cognition; Sacks,
Schegloff, and Jefferson on turn-taking; Milner and Hoare on process
calculi; Abramsky and colleagues on game semantics; and contemporary
work on computational interaction and computational rationality.
Professional definitions from the International Ergonomics Association,
ACM SIGCHI, American Chemical Society, Ecological Society of America,
Linguistic Society of America, and American Psychological Association
were used only to characterize declared disciplinary objects.

The review sought contradictory evidence in two directions. Evidence
against primary-object status included accounts that reduce interaction
without loss to participant states, communication, cognition, control,
or task performance. Evidence against a new discipline included
frameworks that already supply a complete-enough ontology, mechanisms,
measurement, and prediction. Evidence supporting primary-object status
included relational effects, path dependence, emergence, and
coordination patterns that cannot be inferred from participant
properties considered independently.

This is not an exhaustive database review. Search coverage was broad
across traditions but uneven across languages and publication venues.
The assessment therefore concerns the theoretical record located and the
standards it meets, not the absence of every possible prior formulation.

## 5.2 Major formalization traditions

**Cybernetics and control theory.** Interaction is a closed causal loop
involving plant, controller, sensor, goal/reference, disturbance, and
feedback. Strong on measurable dynamics, stability, adaptation, and
intervention. Weak on semantic interpretation and social constitution
unless supplemented.

**Dynamical systems and coordination dynamics.** Interaction is coupled
evolution in a joint state space. Strong on emergence, synchronization,
phase transitions, and multi-scale time. Weak when state variables lack
independently validated psychological or social meaning.

**Ecological psychology and enactivism.** Interaction is
organism-environment reciprocity; action possibilities are relational
and perception is oriented toward action. Strong on embodiment,
environmental structure, and mutuality. Weak on formal semantics,
institutions, and many computational representations.

**Conversation analysis and interactional linguistics.** Interaction is
sequentially organized social action, including turn construction,
adjacency, preference, repair, and accountability. Strong on endogenous
evidence and public organization. Limited generality beyond socially
interpretable conduct.

**Activity theory, situated action, distributed cognition.** Interaction
is mediated action within historically developed activity systems and
distributed cognitive arrangements. Strong on artifacts, context,
organizational roles, and development. Formal predictive comparison
remains difficult.

**Classical cognitive HCI.** Interaction is decomposed into goals,
operators, methods, selections, perceptual/motor/cognitive processors,
and task structures. Strong on quantitative prediction for practiced
tasks. Limited for open-ended interpretation and social change.

**Computational interaction and computational rationality.** Interaction
is modeled using optimization, probabilistic inference, control, machine
learning, cognitive models, and resource-bounded rationality. Strong on
explicit generative models and design-relevant prediction. Often
requires task-specific objective functions and representations.

**Process calculi, automata, protocol theory, and interaction
machines.** Interaction is communication and state transition among open
processes. Strong on compositional semantics, equivalence, liveness,
safety, and verification. Human meaning and learning are normally
outside scope.

**Game theory and game semantics.** Interaction is strategic or logical
exchange among players/system and environment. Strong on incentives,
information structure, strategies, and compositional meaning.
Assumptions about rationality or formal moves can be restrictive.

**Multi-agent and semantic ontologies.** Interaction is represented
through agents, actions, commitments, roles, messages, services, and
protocols. Strong on machine-readable interoperability. Ontologies
usually encode domain commitments rather than establish natural
scientific mechanisms.

# 6. Comparative Analysis

## 6.1 Does interaction have identity conditions?

Interaction is not individuated merely by temporal co-occurrence. Two
systems may change simultaneously without interacting. A defensible
minimum criterion is counterfactual reciprocal dependence: during a
bounded interval, a state or action of at least one participant alters
the probability distribution over subsequent states, observations, or
actions of another, and the resulting change can in turn condition later
behavior in the joint trajectory. Reciprocity may be delayed,
asymmetric, intermittent, mediated, or distributed; it need not occur in
every adjacent event.

This definition separates interaction from one-way influence while
allowing unequal agency. It also makes boundary selection explicit. An
episode is bounded by an analytic rule such as task onset and
completion, stabilization, conversational sequence closure, loss of
causal coupling, or an experimentally imposed interval. Different
boundaries may be legitimate for different questions, as in ecology and
systems science, but they must be declared and tested for robustness.

The interaction object is therefore a trajectory over a joint state
space, together with the coupling relations that generate it. It is not
a third substance inserted between participants. Its identity resembles
a reaction, conversation, control loop, or ecosystem process: a
structured occurrence whose properties depend on relations and history.

## 6.2 Can interaction-level properties be non-reducible?

Several established phenomena support interaction-level description.
Coordination can exhibit synchrony, entrainment, turn organization,
escalation, deadlock, mutual adaptation, repair, and convention
formation. These properties are not attributes of either participant
alone. They depend on coupling topology, timing, information
availability, and history. Knowing each participant\'s isolated
capability is generally insufficient to determine the joint trajectory.

Non-separability does not prove ontological independence. The joint
behavior may still be explained completely by participant models plus
coupling laws. In physics, a collision is a legitimate object of
analysis even when the theory reduces it to fields and particles.
Likewise, interaction can be primary for measurement and explanation
without requiring fundamental primitives beyond agents, environments,
states, actions, and coupling.

The strongest case for theoretical independence would be a law or
mechanism whose explanatory role cannot be preserved by any reasonable
decomposition into participant dynamics and established coupling
theories. The reviewed literature does not yet provide such a result.
Emergence, path dependence, and circular causation motivate
interaction-level models, but systems theory, cybernetics, dynamical
systems, ecology, and social interaction research already accommodate
them.

# 7. Arguments Supporting Interaction as a Scientific Object

**Relational objects are scientifically legitimate.** Ecology treats
relationships among organisms and environments as its declared object;
cybernetics treats feedback and communication as central; linguistics
studies structured relations between form, meaning, and use. Scientific
objects need not be material substances.

**The trajectory contains information absent from snapshots.** Sequence,
latency, mutual adaptation, repair, and path dependence cannot be
reconstructed from static participant attributes alone. The ordering of
events can change outcomes even when initial and final states match.

**Interaction supports intervention.** Researchers can manipulate
coupling strength, feedback delay, action availability, information
asymmetry, representation, incentives, and protocol rules, then measure
predicted changes in trajectories. This supports causal rather than
merely descriptive inquiry.

**Interaction admits multiple formal representations.** State-transition
systems, differential equations, stochastic processes, process algebras,
games, information-theoretic measures, and generative cognitive models
can represent interaction without dependence on a particular interface.

**Cross-domain regularities are plausible.** Feedback, coordination,
exploration, adaptation, breakdown, repair, and convention formation
recur in biological, social, and computational systems. Whether these
regularities support a unified theory is an empirical question, but they
define a coherent comparative program.

**Observer participation can be modeled.** Second-order cybernetics,
ethnomethodology, and measurement theory show that observer dependence
need not invalidate an object. It requires explicit boundary,
instrument, coding, and reflexivity assumptions.

# 8. Arguments Against

**Excessive extension.** If interaction includes every causal relation,
the object becomes coextensive with science and loses discriminatory
value. A domain must restrict participants, coupling, scale, and
explanatory questions.

**Redescription without mechanism.** Calling a sequence an interaction
does not explain it. Established mechanisms - perception, learning,
control, communication, incentives, norms, and computation - may already
provide all causal content.

**Observer-imposed boundaries.** Episodes, agents, and environments may
be analytically selected rather than naturally segmented. Different
choices can produce different interaction descriptions and apparent
laws.

**Heterogeneous mechanisms.** Molecular interaction, social negotiation,
and human-computer use may share only an abstract pattern of mutual
influence. A theory general enough to include all may become too weak to
predict any.

**Existing integrative frameworks.** Systems theory, cybernetics,
ecology, cognitive science, distributed cognition, and HCI already study
organized relations across components. A new framework must beat their
principled combination, not isolated caricatures.

**No demonstrated predictive increment.** The literature contains strong
local models but no accepted benchmark in which a general interaction
theory yields replicated out-of-domain predictions superior to
integrated existing models.

**Institutional criteria are not scientific criteria.** A journal,
program, ontology, or professional identity can be useful without
establishing a new natural kind or autonomous explanatory domain.

# 9. Attempted Falsification: Are Existing Theories Already Complete?

The strongest negative hypothesis is that an interaction episode is
fully explained by: (1) participant state-transition models; (2) an
environment model; (3) communication and observation channels; (4)
coupling rules; and (5) established mechanisms for learning, control,
decision, and social organization. On this view, "interaction" is a
convenient projection of an already complete joint model.

Formal computer science supports much of this hypothesis. Open systems
can be modeled as automata or processes whose observable behavior is
defined through communication with an environment. Process calculi
provide composition, equivalence, safety, and liveness. Game semantics
gives meaning through structured exchanges between system and context.
Control and dynamical systems provide joint trajectories and feedback.
Reinforcement learning and partially observable decision processes
provide action, observation, reward, belief update, and adaptation.
Multi-agent systems add strategic dependence and communication.

Behavioral and social theories fill additional gaps. Ecological
psychology supplies relational action possibilities; cognitive science
supplies perception, memory, inference, and learning; conversation
analysis supplies sequence organization and repair; activity theory
supplies motive, mediation, rules, community, and development;
distributed cognition supplies representational coordination across
people and artifacts; human factors supplies joint performance and
safety; HCI provides domain methods and empirical phenomena.

Taken together, these frameworks can express every item in the candidate
ontology. This is substantial evidence against an independent
discipline. Yet expression is not equivalent to a completed explanation.
The frameworks use incompatible boundaries, units, semantics, and
validation practices. Their combination is usually verbal rather than
generative, and it is rarely tested as a composite baseline. The
completeness claim therefore remains unproven in the same way as the
independence claim.

A decisive falsification program would construct the strongest
integrated baseline available, not compare a new theory against one
narrow field. If participant-plus-coupling models predict interaction
trajectories, transfer, breakdown, and intervention outcomes across
domains as well as any interaction-centered theory with equal or lower
complexity, the independent-framework hypothesis should be rejected. If
systematic residuals remain and require a stable new mechanism, the
positive case strengthens.

# 11. Minimum Formal Object

At a declared time scale, let an interaction model contain agents A =
{a1 \... an}, an environment E, a joint external state x_t,
agent-specific observations o_i,t, actions u_i,t, internal or learned
states h_i,t, and a transition rule P(x\_{t+1} \| x_t, u_1,t \... u_n,t,
E_t). Observation functions map joint state to what each agent can
detect. Policies map observations, internal state, goals, and
constraints to action distributions. Learning rules update persistent
agent state. Context variables specify social, institutional,
linguistic, and physical conditions not otherwise included.

An interaction episode is a bounded trajectory I = \<x_0, o_0, u_0, x_1,
\... x_T\> plus the declared coupling structure. It is interactive when
cross-component dependence exceeds a specified null model: at least one
participant\'s action or state changes the conditional distribution of
another participant\'s later observations, states, or actions, and the
resulting trajectory contains a return path or reciprocal dependence
within the episode or across linked episodes.

This template is intentionally compatible with control systems,
stochastic games, dynamical systems, process models, cognitive
architectures, conversation sequences, and qualitative process tracing.
It does not assert that every meaningful variable is numerical. It does
require that each proposed variable have a stated observation or
inference rule.

The model\'s scientific value lies in comparisons. Candidate
interaction-level variables - coordination regime, repair state,
convention, coupling strength, or path-dependent joint strategy - must
improve held-out prediction or causal explanation beyond participant and
environment variables. Otherwise they are descriptive summaries rather
than indispensable constructs.

# 12. Required Empirical Program

1.  **Boundary robustness:** Test whether conclusions survive reasonable
    changes in episode boundary, agent definition, and temporal grain.

2.  **Composite baseline comparison:** Compare interaction-centered
    models against the strongest integrated model from cognition,
    control, communication, learning, social organization, and system
    dynamics.

3.  **Cross-representation invariance:** Hold task and semantic content
    constant while changing visual, auditory, linguistic, or physical
    representation. Determine which trajectory properties transfer.

4.  **Cross-system prediction:** Fit models in one computational system
    and predict behavior in another without redefining core constructs.

5.  **Longitudinal co-adaptation:** Measure human learning,
    computational adaptation, and environmental change jointly rather
    than treating one side as stationary.

6.  **Intervention on coupling:** Manipulate feedback delay,
    observability, initiative, constraints, or communication topology
    and test predicted regime changes.

7.  **Interaction-level residual test:** After modeling participant
    states and coupling, test whether stable residual structure remains
    that is captured by an interaction-specific construct.

8.  **Measurement invariance:** Test whether constructs retain
    comparable meaning across cultures, languages, expertise levels,
    occupations, and access conditions.

9.  **Failure criterion:** Reject the dedicated framework when
    established composite models achieve equal or better prediction,
    intervention selection, and explanatory compression with fewer
    assumptions.

# 13. Threats to Validity

Conceptual breadth is the principal threat. Interaction ranges from
physical force exchange to social meaning-making. The paper mitigates
this by restricting its operational conclusion to bounded reciprocal
causal trajectories and by requiring domain declarations. The
abstraction may still conceal mechanisms too heterogeneous for a common
theory.

Source selection may favor traditions that already formalize
interaction. Important work in anthropology, sociology, communication,
robotics, organizational science, biology, and philosophy may not be
fully represented. The conclusion is therefore calibrated to the
evidence reviewed, not to an assertion of exhaustive coverage.

Disciplinary descriptions are simplified. Every listed field contains
competing ontologies and methods. The comparison identifies dominant
scientific structures rather than legislating a single correct
definition.

Formal tractability can bias the analysis toward measurable
state-transition accounts. Meaning, normativity, identity, power, and
historical development may resist compact formalization while remaining
causally indispensable. The proposed empirical program therefore permits
qualitative and mixed observation models, provided their inferential
warrants are explicit.

Theoretical independence is difficult to prove negatively. Failure to
find an irreducible mechanism does not show that none exists.
Conversely, fragmentation and lack of integration do not prove that one
does. The paper treats both claims as open to comparative testing.

The use of human-computational interaction as the motivating domain may
overstate the generality of conclusions about interaction in other
sciences. The proposed object definition is general, but the recommended
research program should be evaluated first in domains where human and
computational processes reciprocally adapt.

# 14. Discussion

The evidence resolves the central ambiguity by separating focality from
fundamentality. Interaction can be focal because scientists can identify
episodes, manipulate coupling, measure trajectories, and test
mechanisms. It need not be fundamental in the metaphysical sense, and it
need not displace agents, environments, cognition, communication, or
computation. Reactions are primary objects for chemistry without being
independent of physics; ecosystems are primary objects for ecology
despite being composed of biological and physical entities. Scientific
usefulness depends on explanatory organization, not ontological
isolation.

Interaction becomes scientifically distinctive when the explanandum is
the organization of mutual influence over time: why a trajectory
stabilizes, diverges, coordinates, breaks down, repairs, transfers, or
changes its own future coupling. These questions cannot be answered by
listing interface events or participant attributes. They require joint
state, information availability, action opportunity, feedback, history,
and multi-scale adaptation.

Yet the ontology needed to ask those questions is not novel. Established
theories already supply agents, environments, states, representations,
affordances or action sets, actions, transitions, observations,
feedback, adaptation, communication, strategy, context, and evidence.
The scientific deficit is not missing nouns. It is the absence of a
shared comparative framework in which different mechanisms make
commensurable predictions about the same trajectories.

This finding argues against premature discipline formation. A new
discipline would impose institutional boundaries before theoretical
boundaries are demonstrated. It could encourage renaming established
concepts, weaken engagement with mature methods, and make negative
evidence harder to recognize. A dedicated theoretical framework is a
smaller and more falsifiable commitment. It can remain inside HCI and
cognitive science while drawing formal machinery from systems, control,
ecology, linguistics, and computer science.

The framework should earn independence only through residual explanatory
success. It must identify interaction-level variables that are stable
across implementations, operationally distinct from participant
variables, and predictively useful after strong baselines are included.
The central benchmark is not whether a new vocabulary can describe more
phenomena. It is whether it predicts or explains them with greater
accuracy, transfer, or parsimony.

# 15. Conclusion

Interaction can be treated as a primary scientific phenomenon. It has
identifiable instances when defined as a bounded trajectory of
reciprocal causal influence; it produces observable events; it supports
measurement, intervention, mechanism, prediction, and falsification; and
mature sciences demonstrate that relations and processes can
legitimately serve as primary objects.

The evidence does not support an autonomous science of interaction at
present. Existing disciplines collectively provide the relevant
entities, mechanisms, formalisms, and methods. Their integration is
incomplete, but incompleteness of synthesis is not evidence of
theoretical independence. No reviewed framework demonstrates a uniquely
interaction-specific mechanism or replicated predictive increment over a
strong composite baseline.

The justified scientific action is therefore to treat interaction as the
focal explanandum of a dedicated comparative theoretical program. That
program should use established terminology, define interaction episodes
and observation models precisely, compare formal alternatives on shared
data, and accept elimination if integrated existing theories prove
sufficient.
