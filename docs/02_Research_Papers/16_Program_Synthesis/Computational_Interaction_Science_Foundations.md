**Computational Interaction Science**

*Foundations of a Scientific Theory of Computational Interaction*

Final synthesis of the foundational research program

**Final determination: Computational Interaction Science is best
understood as an interdisciplinary synthesis.**

# Abstract

This paper integrates a sequence of foundational studies concerning
scientific objects, representation, ontology, semantics, interpretation,
opportunity, interaction, behavior, measurement, similarity, transfer,
strategy, formal modeling, prediction, and comparative evaluation. Its
purpose is to determine whether the accumulated evidence justifies
Computational Interaction Science as an independent scientific
discipline. The synthesis finds that computational interaction is a
legitimate primary explanandum with a coherent ontology, explicit
boundary conditions, formal representations, measurement contracts,
falsifiable hypotheses, and a cumulative empirical program. The
framework also contributes real explanatory organization by separating
target from representation, semantic structure from interpretation,
system-relative opportunity from agent-relative belief, interaction from
behavior, records from evidence, and similarity from equivalence.
However, the principal mechanisms remain substantially inherited from
established disciplines: coupling and feedback from cybernetics and
control; perception-action relations from ecological psychology;
cognition and learning from cognitive science; formal transition and
process semantics from computer science; authorization from security;
measurement from psychometrics and metrology; and situated, distributed,
and institutional organization from HCI, activity theory, and social
science. The program has not yet demonstrated stable laws, unique
mechanisms, autonomous methods, or predictive gains that cannot be
obtained by disciplined integration of these fields. The evidence
therefore supports Computational Interaction Science as an
interdisciplinary scientific synthesis and research program, not yet as
an independent discipline.

# 1. Problem and Decision Rule

The central question is not whether computational interaction is
important, measurable, or theoretically rich. It is whether scientific
understanding materially improves by recognizing a distinct discipline
rather than treating the subject as an integrated research program
within and across existing sciences.

The decision must separate three claims that are often conflated:
interaction can be a scientific object; a coherent theory of interaction
can exist; and an autonomous discipline is necessary. The first two may
be true while the third remains unsupported.

The discipline claim is accepted only if the research program exhibits a
sufficiently distinct explanatory object, ontology, mechanisms, formal
and empirical methods, predictive or explanatory gains, cumulative
problem structure, and boundaries that cannot be supplied adequately
through integration of existing disciplines. Mere terminological unity,
institutional convenience, or breadth of application is insufficient.

# 2. Method of Synthesis

The synthesis treats each preceding paper as a constrained contribution
rather than as independent proof of the final conclusion. Definitions,
formal contracts, and empirical claims were compared for consistency;
duplicated constructs were normalized to established vocabulary;
conflicts were retained where they mark genuine theoretical
alternatives; and each proposed novelty was tested against reduction to
adjacent theories.

The final falsification test asks whether the complete framework can be
reconstructed from existing disciplines without significant explanatory
loss. If it can, a new discipline is not scientifically necessary. If
integration fails because a distinctive object requires unique
mechanisms, methods, or predictions, disciplinary independence becomes
defensible.

-   Object test: Is computational interaction a stable and
    discriminating explanatory target?

-   Ontology test: Does the framework define a coherent set of entities
    and relations without unnecessary duplication?

-   Mechanism test: Are any generating mechanisms distinctive rather
    than borrowed or recombined?

-   Measurement test: Can constructs be operationalized with
    reproducible evidence and uncertainty?

-   Formal test: Can the theory be represented compositionally and
    analyzed?

-   Prediction test: Does it support scoped, testable forecasts and
    intervention claims?

-   Necessity test: Does the synthesis explain phenomena that strong
    integrated baselines cannot?

# 3. Explanatory Object

The primary explanatory object is not the interface, the user, the
device, the software product, or isolated behavior. It is the
computational interaction episode: a bounded interval in which
distinguishable systems are coupled such that perturbations alter
another system\'s possible or actual trajectory and the resulting
configuration reciprocally or recurrently constrains a later trajectory
within a declared boundary and timescale.

This definition is strict enough to distinguish interaction from one-way
causation, isolated action, passive observation, mere co-presence, and
arbitrary input-output succession. Reciprocity may be asymmetric,
delayed, probabilistic, environmentally mediated, or distributed over a
sequence. Intentionality and communication are not necessary.

Behavior is downstream but not synonymous: it is a bounded, temporally
extended trajectory of observable or instrumentally recoverable changes,
outputs, or regulated persistence attributed to a system under a
declared observation model. A record is not behavior until detection,
attribution, boundary selection, and interpretation have been made
explicit.

# 5. Dependency Architecture

The framework is relational rather than a one-way pipeline.
Nevertheless, an analytical dependency order is required to prevent
category errors:

**Domain and ontology -\> semantic structure -\> representation and
projection\
-\> observation and interpretation -\> opportunity profile -\>
interaction episode\
-\> behavioral trace -\> strategy and adaptation -\> measurement,
comparison, and prediction**

Feedback operates in the reverse direction. Behavior changes state;
outcomes change beliefs and strategies; repeated interaction changes
experience; institutions alter permissions and representations;
measurement choices alter what becomes visible as evidence. The arrows
therefore mark analytical dependence, not deterministic causation.

# 6. Foundational Assumptions

1.  Targets and representations are distinguishable. A representation is
    never assumed identical to what it represents.

2.  Equivalence is typed. Semantic, informational, structural,
    opportunity, behavioral, strategic, and measurement equivalence are
    distinct relations.

3.  Interaction boundaries, participants, state variables, and
    timescales are analytical commitments and must be declared.

4.  Representation does not determine behavior. Effects are mediated by
    interpretation, opportunity, goals, capabilities, history, and
    context.

5.  System-relative availability and agent-relative belief are
    different. Feasibility, permission, exposure, signaling, belief,
    execution, and success must be separated.

6.  Records are not self-interpreting. Observation, event detection,
    attribution, segmentation, and construct inference require explicit
    models.

7.  Latent constructs are hypotheses supported by converging evidence,
    not properties read directly from traces.

8.  Prediction is scoped and probabilistic. Predictability depends on
    target, horizon, information set, population, context, model class,
    and tolerated error.

9.  Comparison requires a declared common frame. Incomparability is a
    valid result.

10. Existing terminology and mechanisms are preferred unless explanatory
    loss can be demonstrated.

# 9. Formal Architecture

The recommended formal foundation is a layered observation-enriched
probabilistic labeled transition system rather than a new calculus.

**M = \<N, S, A, T, I, F, C, P, X, Sigma, O, Y, B, G, Mu, R\>**

-   N: participants and roles; S: factored system and environment
    states; A: typed actions and events; T: deterministic or
    probabilistic transition relation.

-   I and F: episode initiation and completion conditions; C:
    capability; P: permission; X: projection exposure; Sigma: signaling.

-   O: participant observation model; Y: instrument record model; B:
    belief state; G: goals or evaluation criteria; Mu: strategy or
    policy; R: recovery and reversibility relations.

-   Process-algebra operators supply composition, synchronization,
    hiding, and relabeling; event structures or Petri-net views preserve
    concurrency, conflict, and resource structure.

-   Equivalence and similarity remain typed: trace, simulation,
    bisimulation, testing, semantic, opportunity, behavioral, strategic,
    outcome, and measurement-relative relations are not interchangeable.

The architecture is sufficient as a research representation because it
separates system dynamics, projection, epistemic state, action
selection, measurement, and recovery while permitting mappings to
established formalisms. It is not evidence of a novel mathematical
foundation.

# 10. Evidence and Measurement Framework

The empirical system of record is a three-contract chain:

**Interaction Contract -\> Behavioral Evidence Contract -\> Measurement
Contract**

-   The Interaction Contract declares participants, boundaries, states,
    coupling, preconditions, events, transitions, protocol, invariants,
    failure, recovery, completion, and uncertainty.

-   The Behavioral Evidence Contract declares records, detection rules,
    attribution, segmentation, trace ordering, alternative explanations,
    and inferential limits.

-   The Measurement Contract declares construct, measurand, unit,
    instrument, operationalization, transformation, scale, uncertainty,
    validity evidence, population, context, and prohibited
    interpretation.

The canonical evidence ladder is: raw record; verified observation;
coded observation; derived measure; model-based estimate; construct
evidence; mechanism evidence; generalized claim. Every upward transition
requires additional assumptions and possible falsifiers.

# 11. Prediction

Computational interaction supports predictive science only in a bounded
sense. Low-level and well-specified phenomena such as motor time, choice
under constrained alternatives, practiced task time, near-term
transitions, and localized risks can support quantitative forecasts.
Discovery, error, recovery, and abandonment are predictable
probabilistically when state, task, projection, history, and population
are adequately represented.

Long-horizon adaptation, transfer, expertise, and strategy are weaker
targets because latent state, nonstationarity, multiple viable policies,
social context, and changing opportunity structures compound
uncertainty. Predictive success does not establish mechanism, and
intervention claims require causal assumptions beyond observational
accuracy.

A valid prediction must declare target, horizon, information set,
population, context, model class, baseline, uncertainty, calibration,
transport scope, and decision consequence.

# 12. Methodological Framework

11. Declare the target phenomenon, episode boundary, participants,
    timescale, and explanatory question.

12. Specify ontology, semantic commitments, state variables, and
    admissible transitions.

13. Describe each representation or projection using a projection
    contract.

14. Construct typed opportunity profiles for relevant participants and
    states.

15. Collect records under an explicit observation and instrumentation
    model.

16. Detect and attribute behavioral events without importing latent
    explanations prematurely.

17. Operationalize constructs using measurement contracts and
    uncertainty budgets.

18. Compare interactions only after passing target, boundary,
    correspondence, measurement, and population comparability gates.

19. Fit explanatory and predictive models against strong domain-specific
    and integrated baselines.

20. Test transfer, strategy, and causal claims with appropriate
    counterfactual or intervention designs.

21. Report typed profiles, uncertainty, failures, and incomparability
    rather than forcing universal scores.

22. Update the shared ontology only when an irreducible explanatory gap
    has been demonstrated.

# 15. Explanatory Gain

The synthesis provides genuine explanatory gain in four forms.

-   Disambiguation: it prevents ontology, semantics, representation,
    interpretation, opportunity, behavior, and evidence from being
    collapsed.

-   Cross-level linkage: it connects formal system properties with
    projection, observer access, belief, action, trace, learning, and
    institutional constraint.

-   Comparability: it supplies contracts and typed profiles for
    comparing implementations without assuming informational or semantic
    equivalence entails behavioral equivalence.

-   Research coordination: it converts scattered constructs into
    cumulative artifacts that can be tested, reused, and revised.

These are substantive contributions. They justify a named framework and
research program. They do not yet prove theoretical autonomy because the
gains arise mainly from disciplined integration and boundary management
rather than from a uniquely interaction-specific mechanism.

# 17. Research Agenda

23. Publish stable machine-readable schemas for the Interaction,
    Opportunity, Behavioral Evidence, Measurement, Similarity, Transfer,
    Strategy, Prediction, and Comparison contracts.

24. Create benchmark interaction episodes spanning GUI, command, API,
    conversational, multimodal, accessible, collaborative, and
    safety-critical systems.

25. Develop shared annotation datasets preserving raw records,
    observation models, attributed events, uncertainty, and rival
    interpretations.

26. Test reliability of episode boundaries, opportunity coding, event
    attribution, and strategy inference across laboratories.

27. Evaluate whether typed opportunity and projection variables improve
    explanation and prediction beyond established HCI, cognitive,
    control, and process models.

28. Develop causal experiments separating semantic preservation,
    projection, signaling, capability, permission, and belief.

29. Test transfer and adaptation across representation, modality,
    device, task, and institutional context using source-target
    similarity contracts.

30. Formalize compositional mappings among transition systems, event
    structures, process algebra, probabilistic models, and measurement
    traces.

31. Establish non-compensatory criteria for accessibility, safety,
    authorization, and irreversible harm in comparative evaluation.

32. Conduct preregistered multi-site replications before making
    discipline-level claims.

# 18. Open Problems

-   Boundary ontology: when do analyst-chosen episode boundaries
    correspond to stable causal or functional organization?

-   Reciprocity threshold: what minimum recurrent closure distinguishes
    interaction from extended one-way causation?

-   Emergent action vocabularies: how should open-ended interaction be
    modeled when available actions are constructed during activity?

-   Collective agency: how should participant identity and strategy be
    represented in distributed human-machine organizations?

-   Semantic grounding: under what conditions can semantic state be
    specified independently of interpretive practice?

-   Opportunity measurement: can believed, signaled, and recoverable
    action relations be estimated reliably across populations and
    modalities?

-   Mechanism identification: can traces distinguish competing
    cognitive, ecological, institutional, and computational mechanisms?

-   Nonstationarity: how should models remain valid when participants,
    systems, policies, and conventions co-adapt?

-   Ethics and power: how should coercion, manipulation, exclusion, and
    institutional authority enter the ontology without being reduced to
    preference or performance?

-   Disciplinary threshold: what empirical achievements would convert an
    interdisciplinary synthesis into a scientifically autonomous field?

# 20. Conclusion

The research program has crossed the threshold from a collection of
intuitions to a coherent scientific framework. It possesses a legitimate
explanatory object, cumulative ontology, first principles, formal
representation, measurement architecture, falsifiable claims,
comparative method, and empirical agenda. It can support rigorous
science.

The evidence does not, however, establish the necessity of an
independent discipline. The framework\'s strongest achievements are
synthesis, disambiguation, cross-level linkage, and methodological
coordination. Its mechanisms, mathematical tools, measurement theories,
and empirical methods remain substantially inherited from HCI, cognitive
science, psychology, ecological psychology, cybernetics, control,
computer science, information science, learning science, and social
theory. Predictive and explanatory gains over strong integrated
baselines have been proposed but not yet demonstrated through a
cumulative empirical record.

**Final determination 2: Computational Interaction Science is best
understood as an interdisciplinary synthesis.**

This conclusion is not a rejection of the framework. It identifies its
scientifically defensible status. The appropriate next step is not
disciplinary declaration but empirical competition: the synthesis should
be tested against the best combined explanations available from adjacent
sciences. Independent disciplinary recognition would become justified
only if that program demonstrates durable mechanisms, methods,
predictions, or explanatory successes that integration alone cannot
supply.
